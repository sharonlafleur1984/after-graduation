# Business Evaluation

**Last updated:** September 26, 2026

**Bottom line:** it's a good design aimed at a real need, but it isn't a business yet. The direction: a free, self-serve planner for families, with money from optional extras, schools or funders. Run three cheap tests first, then decide. The decision date lives on the [Roadmap](Roadmap#milestones).

**How to read this page:** every source linked here was checked. Only the exceptions are marked, as "partly verified" or "disagrees." The full check is on [Sources](Sources).

## Who it's for: every high school student, with an optional IEP layer

The planner works for every student. The IEP pieces (accommodations, IEP dates, disability aid, and Vocational Rehabilitation, the state agency that helps people with disabilities find work) are an optional layer turned on in settings.

**Why it matters:** a much bigger audience, but a more crowded market. The IEP layer is what makes it stand out, not the whole product.

## Is the need real? Yes. Demand for a tool is not proven yet.

Many students don't take the standard path, and the ones who don't get the least help.

<details>
<summary><b>The evidence</b></summary>

- 37.2% of 2024 graduates weren't in college that fall, and about a third of those who were attended 2-year schools ([BLS, Apr 2025](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/hsgec.nr0.htm)).
- About 1 million of the 5.1 million college awards each year are certificates below a bachelor's degree ([NCES](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/coe/indicator/cts)).
- About 4 in 10 seniors don't finish the FAFSA, the federal student aid form. Completion hit a record 59.1% for the class of 2026 ([The College Investor, reporting NCAN](https://thecollegeinvestor.com/83916/class-of-2026-sets-fafsa-completion-record-at-59-1-ncan-reports/), partly verified, secondary source).
- Students with IEPs are about twice as likely to plan on a path shorter than a 4-year degree, 25% vs 14% ([NLTS 2012, IES](https://ies.ed.gov/sites/default/files/migrated/nces_pubs/ncee/pubs/20174016/pdf/20174017.pdf), partly verified: we found the report, not this table).
- Families lack information about accommodations after high school ([GAO-24-105614](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-105614)).
- Job training for students with disabilities (Pre-Employment Transition Services, or Pre-ETS, paid for by Vocational Rehabilitation) reaches only a small share of eligible students. Sources disagree on how small: about 4.4% ([NCLD 2026](https://ncld.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/VR-and-Pre-ETS-White-Paper.pdf)) or about 10% ([Hechinger Report, Feb 2025](https://hechingerreport.org/hundreds-of-thousands-of-students-are-entitled-to-training-and-help-finding-jobs-they-dont-get-it/)). Use a range.

**Caveat:** nobody has yet found parents or counselors saying "I wish this tool existed." The need is proven. Demand for a tool is not.

</details>

## Who else does this? Many tools, but none give families trade paths and a personal timeline

**The gap that still looks open:** no free product for families that (1) gives trade and certificate paths equal weight, (2) builds a dated personal timeline, and (3) explains why each school fits this student. Plus the optional IEP layer, which only CollegeCountdown partly covers.

## Is it worth building? Not as a big startup. Three smaller routes need evidence.

| If the goal is... | Verdict | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| A big venture-funded company | Don't build | Medium-high |
| A free self-serve planner, with paid extras for families | Needs more evidence (cheapest to test) | Moderate |
| A paid tool for schools | Needs more evidence | Moderate |
| A free, grant-funded tool | Needs more evidence | Low-moderate |

<details>
<summary><b>Why</b></summary>

**What's strong**
- The need, and the design: thoughtful, accessible, plain language
- Real accessibility work (93 ARIA attributes, reduced-motion support)
- The front end is mostly done. A product is mainly back-end and content work.

**What's missing**
- Proof that anyone pays: families, schools or funders
- All content today was written for one student. Self-serve onboarding has to make each new student automatic, with no work from Sharon.
- Keeping aid rules and deadlines accurate every year is the ongoing cost

**Why not a big startup**
- Even with every junior and senior in the US at district prices ($3 to $6 per student), that's about $22M to $44M a year, and only at 100% market share. Claude's math: 7.37M students × $3 to $6.
- Edtech investment has dropped sharply, to less than $3 billion in 2025 ([Rest of World, 2026](https://restofworld.org/2026/edtech-funding-collapse-k12-startups-ai-workforce/), partly verified).
- Statewide contracts already lock up many districts. Utah buys a statewide planning platform ([YouScience](https://www.youscience.com/resources/press/utah-selects-youscience-as-statewide-platform/)) and lists a free transition tool ([Utah SSIP 2025](https://schools.utah.gov/specialeducation/_specialeducation/_datareporting/_apr-spp-ssip/_ssipevaluationplan/Data2025SSIP2025Update.pdf)).

**Ads or selling data are off the table,** especially for disability data. Examples of the risk: the [Naviance $17.25M settlement](https://www.k12dive.com/news/what-the-1725m-naviance-settlement-means-for-school-districts/816496/) and the [PowerSchool breach](https://www.security.org/identity-theft/breach/powerschool/).

</details>

## The plan: three cheap tests, then decide

None of the tests needs new software. They run side by side.

**Why self-serve:** a paid subscription is easy to copy now that AI makes building apps easy, and Sharon herself wouldn't pay $79 to $99 a year. What's hard to copy is up-to-date, checked state and school information. So the planner is free, and each new family costs almost nothing.

| Test | What you do | It passes if... |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Families | Ask in the usability sessions which paid extras families want and what feels fair. Then offer the top one to 10 families (5 with an IEP or 504 plan, 5 without) as a pre-order with a refundable deposit. | 3 or more place a deposit |
| 2. Schools | Ask 3 to 5 transition coordinators what budget they'd pay from, and offer a free one-year pilot to one or two districts ([Backlog idea 19](Backlog)) | At least one district takes the pilot and names a real budget for year two, at $15 or more per student (or $3K or more per district) |
| 3. Grant funding | Find one funder and one group to keep each state's content up to date | Both exist, at about $11K to $21K per state per year for every 1,000 students |

**Then decide:** build at most one route, in one state, and only if its test passes. If none pass, stop. The tool stays a personal planner. How the family test runs: [Research Plan](Research-Plan#study-2-paid-extras).

<details>
<summary><b>The money math</b></summary>

- **Free planner for families:** each new family costs almost nothing, because onboarding does the setup. The real cost is keeping each state's content accurate every year (see the grant math below). Extras only need to cover that.
- **Extras:** price not set. The usability sessions ask what feels fair first.
- **School license:** needs $15 or more per student across 5 to 10 districts per state. Nothing in the market proves that price yet.
- **Free grant-funded version:** costs a funder about $11K to $21K per state per year for every 1,000 students, or $25K to $45K in year one if the build is paid up front. No funder has been found yet.
- **Cost of the tests:** about 60 to 150 hours of Sharon's time plus under $1,000.
- All of these are estimates at an assumed $50 an hour, not forecasts.

**Prices we compared against**
- Scoir: $4.80 per high school student per year ([Scoir pricing](https://www.scoir.com/middle-and-high-schools/pricing))
- Xello: $4.45 per high school student (list $5.45), 3-year term ([Park Hill SD quote, 2025](https://boe.parkhill.k12.mo.us/attachments/31a77d78-bb41-47aa-a5bd-5d3b8813bf31.pdf))
- TAGG transition assessment: $5 per set ([Zarrow Institute](https://tagg.ou.edu/tagg/manual/overview))
- IEP Compass parent app: $79.99 a year ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/iep-compass-parent-advocate/id6761731246))
- Private admissions consultants: about $4,000 to $12,000 for a full package ([Dewey Smart, 2026](https://www.deweysmart.com/resources/how-much-does-college-admissions-consulting-cost-2026-pricing-guide), partly verified, secondary source)
- Private educational consultants: "just under $140 an hour" ([IECA](https://www.iecaonline.com/student-parent-information/what-is-an-independent-educational-consultant/how-to-choose-the-right-independent-educational-consultant/), partly verified: no dollar figure on the page we found)

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Don't say this</b>: claims the evidence doesn't support. Check any outside pitch against this list.</summary>

- "A student is using it today"
- "Plugs the Pre-ETS leak"
- "Competitors don't compare schools or cost" (Trinity does)
- "Counselors are overloaded (372:1)." High school ratios now meet the recommended 250:1 ([ASCA, Feb 2026](https://www.schoolcounselor.org/getmedia/62807f33-a020-4c4f-ac6f-bf284803fd97/pr_ratios-24-25.pdf)).
- Pre-ETS money described as sitting unused ([RSA reallotment](https://rsa.ed.gov/about/programs/vocational-rehabilitation-state-grants/reallotment-information))
- "Free tools cap the price"
- Utah's ~$9.33 per student described as a contract price. It comes from a budget request ([Utah Legislature, 2025](https://le.utah.gov/interim/2025/pdf/00001679.pdf), partly verified).
- 4.1M students aged 14 to 21. Use about 2.4M.

</details>
